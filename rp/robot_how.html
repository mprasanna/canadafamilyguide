The execution model

Forty-four weeks. Forty-four visible outputs.

The total length is not the number to stare at. The interval between seeing something is.

Weekly hours
20 — roughly 10–12 across weekday evenings, 8–10 at the weekend
Total
≈880 hours across 44 weeks
At 8 h/week
The same programme becomes 18–24 months and probably does not finish
Session rule
Every session ends with a commit. Nothing uncommitted overnight.
Protected time
One hour weekly for decision records, the engineering log, and footage
Change control
Frozen baseline. Changes need evidence from a build or a test, recorded as a decision record.

01 — Weekly artifact

The unit of progress is the week, not the phase.

Phase gates sit six to ten weeks apart — too long to sustain solo work. So each week ends with one thing that can be looked at: thirty seconds of screen capture, a plot, a photograph, a results table. Committed with a one-line note. It is not a deliverable for anyone else; it is evidence the week happened.

The largest consequence is that hardware moves in week 3, not week 14. A single-motor bench rig — one motor, encoder, driver, microcontroller — is ordered in week 1. That does not breach the purchase gates, which exist to prevent buying a complete robot before the software works, not to prevent a test fixture. It also moves the biggest schedule risk, the embedded learning curve, from week 10 to week 4, where discovering a problem costs two weeks instead of eight.

The trap in this

Calibration, failure analysis and documentation are the least photogenic work and the most differentiating. The answer is to make them visible, not to defer them. A calibration procedure that cut odometry error by 60% is a result. A failure report with a graph in it is an artifact.

02 — Six phases, gated

No phase opens until the previous one passes a number.

Foundation & simulation
WK 1–6
Reproducible environment; autonomy loop closed in simulation; bench rig holding commanded velocity within 5%
Drivetrain & firmware
WK 7–14
Safe mobile base: 2 m straight run within 5 cm, wheel speed error ≤5%, timeout stops motors, e-stop cuts power with software running
ROS 2 integration
WK 15–22
C++ hardware interface replaces the simulator plugin; one controller config serves both; odometry within 2% over 10 m
Sensors & autonomy
WK 23–32
Ten hardware goals, zero interventions, ≤10 cm and ≤5°, repeated another day from a fresh map
Reliability
WK 33–40
Three failures measured, diagnosed, corrected, regression-checked — the phase carrying the most signal
Operations & presentation
WK 41–44
An operator can see state, progress and why a mission failed; architecture and reports published; demo recorded

03 — Progress instruments

Two numbers, readable in five seconds.

Criteria tracker
46 numbered acceptance criteria across the six phases. Count complete over 46 is the progress bar for the whole programme.
Metrics log
Every measurement, dated, with its method. The more motivating of the two: criteria tick once, metrics improve every time you touch them.
Monthly review
Three figures — criteria complete, hours averaged, weeks elapsed. Nothing else needs inventing.

Metrics worth trending

Final pose error · wheel velocity steady-state error · velocity step settling time · odometry error over a measured 10 m · map drift and loop-closure error · failure detection latency · time to safe state · recovery time and success rate · mission success rate across sessions · serial frame error rate · motor current at cruise and battery runtime · hours worked per week.

04 — When momentum breaks

The commitment is to finishing, not to a date.

Over forty-four weeks something interrupts — work escalation, illness, coursework, family. The failure mode is not slowing down; it is stopping entirely, because the plan had no state between on track and abandoned. So there is a third state, and it is the only sanctioned way to slow down.

Maintenance cadence
Two hours a week. One session. Keep the repository alive, write one log entry, do the smallest next thing. No new scope, no gate pressure.
Declared, not drifted
Entered explicitly in the log with a date and a reason, reviewed monthly. Undeclared drift is what turns a pause into an ending.
On return
Resume at the phase you were in. The end date moves; the criteria and the scope do not.
Hard limit
Nothing here is worth sleep, health or family time. A pace that costs those is unsustainable by definition — and unsustainable is the most reliable way not to finish.

Rules that keep it on track

  1. Robot depth first. The operations layer stays small and advisory, permanently.
  2. No phase begins until the previous one meets its numeric gate.
  3. Every failure becomes evidence: bag, logs, hypothesis, measurement, cause, fix, regression check.
  4. Safety is independent of software. The hardware e-stop removes motor power regardless of firmware or ROS 2 state.
  5. C++ for control-adjacent components. Python only where timing and production constraints permit.
  6. No arm, no second robot, no digital twin, no model in any control path, in version 1.
  7. Documentation is continuous, not a final phase.
  8. One visible artifact every week. A week without one is a week to examine, not to excuse.
  9. The baseline is frozen. It changes on evidence — never on speculation, and never as a substitute for building.
