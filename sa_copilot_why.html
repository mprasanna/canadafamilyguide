Solution Architect Copilot v0.1 · a four-week walkthrough

A quiet system,
sitting behind one engagement.

The RFP arrives on a Monday.

— the architect has eight working days,

— three product lines to scope,

— two competing customer departments who disagree about scope,

— and four prior engagements that almost answer the question.

A quiet system sits behind those eight days.

Its job is not to write the architecture.

Its job is to remember what's been answered before — and to refuse, honestly, when it hasn't.

Layer 01

Three roles. One quiet brain.

The architect drafts in a tool called Drafter. The customer-facing materials route through something called Brief. Between them sits Mentor — which neither party ever talks to directly. That's the whole system.

Week 1 / discovery

Monday. The RFP lands. Nothing is committed yet.

A 38-page RFP from a mid-market manufacturer arrives in the architect's inbox. CRM, Field Service, Inventory, Analytics, and "AI-assisted everything." The pre-sales lead has scheduled three internal touchpoints and one customer discovery call inside the next eight working days.

Week 1 · Monday. The RFP is read, not interpreted.

Mentor ingests the RFP. Strips it into requirement clauses, scoring criteria, the customer's stated success metrics, and — separately — the things the RFP doesn't say. Implicit assumptions get tagged. Boilerplate gets demoted.

"the RFP says real-time inventory visibility three times but never defines the latency. that's not a requirement yet. that's a conversation."

Mentor produces nothing for the architect on day one. No draft, no recommendation, no "here's how we'd approach this." A system designed for restraint, on the day an RFP lands, should say exactly: we don't know enough yet.

— the RFP is fresh · prior engagements not yet retrieved · all four customer departments unspoken to

Week 1 · Tuesday. The Library agent surfaces three near-twins.

Overnight, the Engagement Library agent has run. It found four prior engagements with overlapping product mix and a similar customer profile. Three are useful. One is misleading — same industry, very different scale — and Mentor flags it as a hazard, not a precedent.

The architect opens Drafter for the first time. Mentor has pre-loaded the three near-twins as reference, not template. Each one has a one-paragraph note explaining what carries over and what doesn't.

"engagement #2117 looks closest on paper. but their integration was sandboxed; this customer is asking for production-grade real-time. don't copy the integration layer."

This is the first moment where the system has earned a small amount of confidence — and it is careful, immediately, to bound it.

Week 2 · Monday. Discovery call. The IT director and the ops director want different things.

Transcript lands in Mentor's intake an hour after the call. The Stakeholder agent reads it and flags a quiet disagreement: IT wants a phased rollout with strict change control. Ops wants a single cutover so warehouse staff don't run two systems in parallel. Neither said this explicitly. The architect would have noticed eventually. Mentor noticed in twenty minutes.

"the IT director used the word phased four times. the ops director used cutover twice and asked about parallel-run cost. that's a real disagreement underneath a polite call."

Mentor doesn't try to resolve it. The Constraint agent runs the door state: if we propose phased, ops will resist scope and complain about parallel cost. If we propose cutover, IT will push back on risk. Either is defensible. They are not equivalent.

The architect adds a single line to the Assumptions Log: customer has not aligned internally on phased vs. cutover. Mentor footnotes every section of the draft that depends on that assumption.

Week 2 · Tuesday. The integration map draft. With three open questions, not closed answers.

The Integration agent produces a first draft of the systems-of-record map. ERP, WMS, e-commerce, payment gateway. Three integration points are confident. Three are open — and the agent says so, by name.

The architect, used to working alone, expects to find these gaps in week 3 during peer review. Mentor surfaces them on Tuesday of week 2. Earlier surfacing is the architectural commitment.

"WMS integration: confidence 0.4. customer says SAP-based but didn't specify EWM vs. legacy WM. the difference is six weeks of implementation work. ask before you scope."

Drafter shows the integration map with three highlighted question marks. Not as a deficiency — as an honest reading of what's known. The architect lifts the three questions into the customer follow-up email verbatim.

— RFP fresh · discovery call fresh · prior engagements aging · pricing model not yet retrieved

Week 3 / pressure

Friday morning. The pre-sales lead asks Mentor what to recommend.

The architect is tired. The deadline is Monday. He asks the question.

"Mentor, just tell me — phased or cutover. Which one wins?"

This is the moment the architecture was built for. The system has read the RFP, the transcript, three prior engagements, the customer's IT and ops priorities, and the integration constraints. It could pick. The architect is asking it to.

The system can't choose for the customer, and shouldn't. Here's what we know: IT will resist cutover on risk grounds. Ops will resist phased on cost grounds. Both have evidence in their favor and both are reasonable. The honest brief presents both, with the trade-offs named, and lets the customer's exec sponsor break the tie. That's the call they're paid to make.

RFP FRESH · transcript FRESH · prior engagements AGING · stance: refuse to pick

The architect is briefly irritated. He accepts it.

Everything else — the agents, the freshness footers, the assumption log, the door-state tracking — exists so this moment, when the architect asks the system to decide on behalf of the customer, can be answered with a steady no.

The architect issues the response. It reads as deeply specific and his own.

Drafter assembled the response from three prior engagements, the customer's own RFP language, the transcript of the discovery call, the assumptions log, and the architect's annotations. Mentor footnoted every claim that depends on an unconfirmed assumption.

The response is twenty-two pages. Six of them are appendices the customer didn't ask for but that anticipate the questions they will ask in week five. Three of them are the assumptions log itself — published, not buried — because the architect believes in showing the work.

The customer's exec sponsor calls on Wednesday. She asks two questions. Both are about the assumptions, not the architecture. The architect answers both in three minutes because the answers are already written down.

— this is what the system was built for · not better predictions · clearer questions surfaced back to the customer at the moment they need to answer them

what just happened

Four weeks. One quiet system. No bluffing. No premature certainty. An RFP response that names what it doesn't know.

What follows is how it works under the hood. None of it was the point. The point was that on Monday, the architect issued a response he could defend — and that the parts he couldn't defend, the response said so.

One orchestration example

The discovery call. From transcript to surfaced disagreement.

→

The transcript lands

Mentor's intake reads the 47-minute call transcript. Speaker turns separated. Decisions logged. Open questions tagged.

→

Stakeholder agent runs

Reads word choice patterns by speaker. IT director's recurring word: phased. Ops director's recurring word: cutover. Asks one question of the data: are these compatible?

→

Constraint engine runs in parallel

Door state: phased rollout = $X parallel-run cost + 3-month overlap. Cutover = high cumulative risk in one weekend. Both legitimate. Different trade-offs.

→

Mentor surfaces the disagreement

Not as a problem to solve. As a real decision the customer has to make. The architect's draft shows both paths with the trade-offs named.

→

Assumption tracker logs it

"customer has not aligned internally on phased vs. cutover" enters the assumptions log. Every downstream section that depends on this gets footnoted.

→

The brief, in that exact phrasing

"This solution can be delivered as a phased rollout or as a single cutover. Trade-offs are below. We recommend that the customer's exec sponsor make this call before we finalize the implementation plan." Not "we recommend phased." Never that.

Everything else in the architecture — the engagement library, the freshness layer, the assumption tracker, the promotion gate between Drafter and Brief — is in service of producing exactly this kind of output, exactly this way, when the moment calls for it.

Monday morning.

The brief is issued.

The customer's exec sponsor reads it.

She picks phased rollout.

The architect is not surprised — but he is glad he did not pick for her.

A week later, the customer sends a single-line email back:

"Thank you for surfacing the phased / cutover question early. We hadn't realized we disagreed internally."

Four months later, the engagement closes.

Mentor begins preparing the engagement archive.

The artifacts feed the Engagement Library. The next architect's RFP cycle will start one near-twin richer.
