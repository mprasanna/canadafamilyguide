Brief · customer-facing · Week 4 · Monday morning

Every SKU line has a justification underneath it. Not "trust us" pricing — sourced to either an RFP clause, a confirmed discovery-call statement, or a named assumption. The customer can audit each one.

The rollout section presents two paths and refuses to pick. The customer's exec sponsor will read this and make the call — which is the call that is actually hers to make. The brief's job is to set the choice up cleanly, not to make it.

The published assumption log is the architectural commitment that most RFP responses bury. The customer learns more from the assumptions than from the numbers — the assumptions are where the next conversation begins.

Drafter · Mentor console · Week 3 · Friday 7:48 am · canonical refusal

The system has every signal it needs to pick. It refuses to. Notice the calm cadence under pressure. No defensiveness. No lecturing. Just door state, stakeholder signal, assumption status, and a clear architectural commitment: picking for the customer's exec sponsor would bypass Gate 1.

This is the canonical refusal — codified after the early pilot engagements surfaced it as the moment the system most often breaks. Mentor has a phrase guide. "The right action is to nudge the exec sponsor for a decision" is canonical. "Recommend phased" is forbidden when a halt is active. The phrasing is the architecture.

Outcome: the architect sends the nudge. The exec sponsor replies over the weekend. The brief promotes Monday morning at 8:42 am (Shot 3) with the phased path selected. Four months later, the engagement closes — and the customer sends back a single-line email that the early surfacing of the disagreement was the moment they realized they hadn't aligned internally.

05 / what crosses, what doesn't

Same architecture. Same observations. Two scopes.

The four mockups above are powered by one shared engagement memory. The boundary between Drafter and Brief isn't a UI mode — it's the promotion gate, enforced at the artifact level. Here is what each surface holds of the same underlying state.

Drafter seesinternal workbench · the architect's view

  • Every confidence value, by section
  • Every halt, with the agent that raised it
  • Every near-twin lookup result, including the hazard flag
  • Full transcript with speaker attribution
  • Pricing midpoint, with the assumptions that would close the band
  • Stakeholder word-choice patterns
  • Mentor console — direct question-and-answer
  • The full assumption log, including unresolved items
— Promotion gate —

Brief seescustomer-facing · post-gate

  • Recommended scope, with per-SKU justification
  • Pricing range — not midpoint — when scope is open
  • Trade-off paths where the customer must choose
  • The published assumption log
  • Confirmed integrations only · paused edges named, not glossed
  • Freshness footer — what's current, what's aging
  • The canonical refusal phrases, where applicable
  • Never · halted sections · unattributed claims · single-number quotes when scope is open

The Friday refusal lives entirely on the left side. What crosses the gate on Monday morning is the trade-off framing, the published assumption log, and the architectural commitment — not the architect's irritation, not Mentor's internal halt banner, and not the speakers' names from the transcript.
