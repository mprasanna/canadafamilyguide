How the data actually moves.

Inputs arrive from three places. They pass through an ephemeral relay into a single canonical engagement memory. Mentor dispatches scoped state to specialists in parallel. Their outputs return, get reconciled under explicit decision rules, and compose into two surfaces with different framings.

02 / canonical engagement memory

One brain. Three kinds of state.

The brain isn't a database. It's an append-only markdown wiki with structured frontmatter — timestamped at ingestion, revision-logged, readable by the architect. Every specialist reads from it. None of them writes to it directly except through a documented path.

01 — Structured

Deterministic facts

RFP, products, prices
  • requirement clauses, by section
  • scoring criteria & weights
  • customer's success metrics
  • product capability inventory
  • pricing tables, by SKU
  • integration patterns library
02 — Narrative

Thematic readings

transcripts, signals, prior engagements
  • stakeholder word-choice patterns
  • unspoken disagreements (flagged)
  • near-twin engagements (with caveats)
  • hazard precedents (do-not-copy)
  • customer team dynamics
  • open questions queue
03 — Governance

Operating rules

how the system holds itself
  • freshness tiers per source
  • confidence values per claim
  • assumption log (live, footnoted)
  • promotion gate audit trail
  • halt authority records
  • revision history
The third column is what most solution-architecture tools leave implicit. Making it first-class state — readable, auditable, timestamped, footnoted into the customer-facing brief — is what makes the orchestration honest. A draft without a visible assumption log is bluffing dressed up as expertise.
03 / the two decision gates

After agents return, two questions are asked in order.

The orchestration's hard part isn't dispatching — it's reconciling outputs that disagree. Mentor uses two gates, in this exact order. The order matters.

Gate 1 Did the Assumption Tracker fire a halt signal?
→ Yes

Downstream specialists' confidence values are clamped. The draft cannot be promoted from Drafter to Brief until the assumption is acknowledged in the customer-facing log. Hard architectural rule. The system stops trying to look certain.

→ No

Continue to Gate 2.

then
Gate 2 Has any single claim's confidence crossed the promotion threshold?
→ Yes

That claim is promoted from "draft observation" to "named recommendation" in the brief. The system has, with deliberate constraint, allowed itself to take a position.

→ No

Claim stays in Drafter as a working note. The brief presents it as an option, not a recommendation. Refuses to choose where the customer must.

then route to surfaces
Routing Drafter (internal) vs. Brief (customer-facing)
→ Drafter

Working draft with every footnote, confidence value, open question, and hazard flag visible. The architect's surface. Refuses nothing.

→ Brief

Customer-facing response. Assumptions log published. Every claim either confidently sourced or honestly flagged. Refuses to choose for the customer.

Order matters because assumption-halt must precede certainty-promotion. Otherwise a high-confidence specialist output could ride past an unresolved assumption and land in the customer brief as a recommendation. The gate sequence is a load-bearing architectural decision.

04 / a canonical run

The Friday morning moment, traced end-to-end.

Same event, from input to output

Two signals from two specialists. One refusal. Twenty minutes of agent time.

Stage 1. Discovery call transcript lands via the architect's intake. 47 minutes. Four customer-side speakers. The Engagement Library has already run overnight and surfaced three near-twin engagements, with one flagged as a hazard precedent.

Stage 2. Transcript lands in narrative state, timestamped, fresh. Speakers attributed. Decisions tagged. Open questions queued.

Stage 3. Mentor classifies: live engagement, draft-shaping phase, customer deadline in three days. Dispatches scoped state to Stakeholder Reader, Constraint Engine, Integration Mapper, and Assumption Tracker.

Stage 4. In parallel: Stakeholder Reader sees recurrent disagreement on rollout cadence — IT director used phased four times, ops director used cutover twice. Constraint Engine recomputes trade-offs — both paths defensible. Integration Mapper has confidence 0.4 on the WMS edge: customer said SAP-based without specifying EWM vs. legacy WM. Assumption Tracker registers two halt conditions.

Stage 5. Mentor's reconciliation. Gate 1: halt signals fired — assumption log gets two new entries (internal-disagreement flag; WMS-platform unknown). Gate 2: no claim crosses the promotion threshold while the WMS assumption is unresolved. Recommendations are demoted to options. The trade-off math is composed for the brief, both paths named.

Stage 6. Composes Drafter under the canonical-phrase guide. Drafter shows the architect: "Two unresolved assumptions block a single recommendation. Trade-offs below. Suggest surfacing the WMS question and the rollout-cadence question to the customer before week 3 commits." The Brief, when later promoted, reads: "This solution can be delivered as phased or cutover; trade-offs below; we recommend the customer's exec sponsor make this call." Not "we recommend cutover." Never that.
